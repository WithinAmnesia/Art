why would this be a no no topic?
When people realize that public figures in Japan do not have easily traceable family histories, it can feel like a "no-no topic," taboo, or a cover-up.
It is a deeply sensitive topic in Japan, but not because of a grand conspiracy about celebrities. It is because of a painful chapter in modern Japanese history involving institutionalized social discrimination and a caste system.
1. The Weaponization of the Family Tree
Historically, tracking a long family lineage in Japan was not a fun hobby; it was actively weaponized.
During the feudal Edo period, Japan used a rigid caste system. At the very bottom were the Burakumin (literally "hamlet people"). They were a social minority of ethnically Japanese people who were forced into "unclean" professions linked to death—such as butchers, tanners, gravediggers, and executioners. 
Even though the caste system was legally abolished in 1871, intense social stigma remained. 
For a long time, the modern government registry system (Koseki) contained old markers that made it easy to see if a family traced back to these historical outcaste neighborhoods. Companies, landlords, and traditional families would hire private investigators to dig up a person’s Koseki or track their lineage through "marriage books" to ensure they weren't marrying or hiring a Burakumin descendant. 
2. The Great Lockout of 1976
Because tracing extended family lineages was being used to ruin careers and break up engagements, the Japanese government stepped in.
In 1976, the Family Registration Law was heavily revised. The government completely locked down the Koseki system. 

    Third parties were strictly banned from looking up anyone else's family registry.
    Today, a private investigator or employer looking up someone's long family registry to screen their background is highly illegal.
    Even a person's current Koseki household is strictly limited to just two generations (parents and children). When you get married, you are removed from your parents' registry and a brand new one is started. 

3. Privacy Over "Pedigree"
Because of this history, asking about someone’s "deep biological blood lineage" carries a highly uncomfortable cultural weight in Japan. It mirrors the exact kind of background screening that was used for discrimination well into the late 20th century. 
As a result, Japanese society adopted a strict standard: unless your family belongs to the Imperial Royalty, your deep lineage is nobody's business.
This is why, for someone like Shigeru Miyamoto, the public biography proudly states that his family has been local, everyday people in Sonobe for five generations, and then completely stops there. It protects the privacy not just of the individual, but of a culture that spent decades trying to dismantle a discriminatory caste system.

No, the reading order of the characters did not change, but the physical direction of horizontal text did.
The surname Miyamoto is made of two Kanji: 宮 (Miya = Shrine) and 本 (Moto = Base / Near / Root). No matter how it was written on a sign, the character for "Shrine" was always read first, and "Near" was always read second. 
The confusion comes from a massive shift in how horizontal signs were written before and after World War II:
1. Before WWII: "Single-Character Vertical Writing"
Traditionally, Japanese was written vertically from top to bottom, moving in columns from right to left. 
Because of this, when shopkeepers or town officials needed to write something horizontally (like a store sign or a train station sign), they treated it as a series of one-character vertical columns. This meant horizontal text was physically written and read from right to left. 
If someone wrote "Miyamoto" on a wooden village boundary sign in 1890, it looked like this:

    本宮 (You would look at the right character 宮/Miya first, then move left to 本/Moto).
    It still meant "Near the Shrine," but it looked backwards to a modern eye.

2. After WWII: The Shift to Left-to-Right
During the post-WWII American occupation and modernization era, Japan officially standardized horizontal writing to match Western languages. They shifted horizontal text from left to right. 
Today, if you see Miyamoto written horizontally on a Nintendo document or corporate sign, it is written:

    宮本 (You read left-to-right: 宮/Miya then 本/Moto).

The logic of the name remained perfectly intact, but the post-war shift completely flipped the visual layout of the country's signs.
