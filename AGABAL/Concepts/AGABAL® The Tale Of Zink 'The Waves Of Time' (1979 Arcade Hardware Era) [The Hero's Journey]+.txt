zelda 1 rings mechanics how they work exactly?
In the original The Legend of Zelda (NES), rings serve as Link’s primary defensive upgrades. Mechanically, they permanently reduce the incoming damage Link takes from all enemies and hazards by fixed fractional percentages, replacing his default green tunic with a new color. 
Here is exactly how the mechanics work:
🛡️ The Two Rings & Damage Reduction

    Green Tunic (Default): Link takes 100% (Full) damage from all incoming hits.
    Blue Ring: Cuts all damage taken by half (50% reduction). You take 1/2 of the normal damage.
    Red Ring: Cuts all damage taken by three-quarters (75% reduction). You take only 1/4 of the normal damage. 

⚙️ Exact Coding & Stacking Rules

    The Effects Do Not Stack: The Red Ring does not stack with the Blue Ring to give you 1/8th damage. Instead, the Red Ring is a hard overwrite of your defense value. It changes your base damage multiplier directly from 1.0 (Green) or 0.5 (Blue) to 0.25 (Red).
    Skipping the Blue Ring: If you decide to skip purchasing the Blue Ring entirely and head straight into Level 9 to grab the Red Ring, you still receive the full 75% damage reduction. Buying the Blue Ring first is not a requirement.
    Buying Sequence Quirks: If you already have the Red Ring and go back to a shop to buy a Blue Ring for 250 Rupees, the game will let you buy it, but it will waste your money. Link's tunic will stay red, and your defense remains at the superior Red Ring tier. 

🎨 The NES Palette Swap Glitch
Because of the strict hardware limitations of the NES, the game cannot generate a completely new sprite color layer for Link. Instead, equipping a ring changes Link's entire global color palette. 
Because certain sprites share the same exact color palette data as Link's tunic, equipping a ring changes the color of other objects in the game: 

    With the Blue Ring, Link's tunic turns white/light blue. Consequently, Princess Zelda’s dress, flying arrows, the raft, shopkeepers, and the whiskers on Pols Voice also turn white/blue.
    With the Red Ring, Link’s tunic turns a brownish-red. All of those same items and characters will instead shift to red. 

📍 Where to Find Them

    Blue Ring: Purchased for 250 Rupees in a secret overworld shop. In the First Quest, it is found by touching the top-middle Armos statue on the screen with six Armos.
    Red Ring: Found deep inside the final dungeon, Level 9 (Death Mountain), in both the First and Second Quests. It is guarded in a room full of Blue Wizzrobes where you must push a specific block to reveal the secret staircase.

While the visual heads-up display (HUD) on your screen only shows health down to a half-heart, the internal programming of The Legend of Zelda (NES) tracks damage with much higher precision. 
The exact mechanics behind how low your damage can go work as follows:
🧮 The Internal 256-Unit Math
Behind the scenes, the game doesn't count damage by whole fractions like quarters or halves. Instead, the game divides each single heart into 256 internal HP units. 

    1 Full Heart = 256 units.
    1/2 Heart = 128 units.
    1/4 Heart = 64 units. 

🛡️ Standard Red Ring Interaction (0.125 Hearts)
Most standard overworld enemies—like Red Octoroks, Moblins, and Tektites—deal 0.5 hearts of base damage, which equals 128 internal units. 
When you equip the Red Ring, it cuts incoming damage down to one-quarter (25%).

Since 32 units is exactly 1/8 of a full heart, your standard minimum damage taken from a basic enemy is 0.125 hearts. 
🐉 The Absolute Lowest Damage (~0.094 Hearts)
There are weird anomalies in the game's code where specific attacks have non-standard base values. For example, Gleeok's physical contact damage is uniquely coded to deal 96 internal units of damage (which is roughly 3/8 of a heart) instead of the usual 128. 
If you get hit by Gleeok's body while wearing the Red Ring, the game quarters that 96 down to 24 internal units.

This is the absolute lowest amount of damage Link can possibly take from a hazard or enemy strike. 
📺 How the HUD Handles It
Because the NES couldn't dynamically render tiny slivers of a heart sprite, the visual health meter is incredibly deceptive. The game only looks at the current byte of your active heart container: 

    If the heart has 128 to 256 units left, it displays as a completely full heart.
    If the heart drops to 1 to 127 units, it instantly snaps to looking like a half heart.
