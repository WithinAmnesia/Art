The symbol you entered is an apostrophe ('), a vital punctuation mark used primarily to indicate possession, create contractions, or show the omission of letters and numbers. 
Primary Uses of the Apostrophe

    Showing Possession: It shows ownership by adding an apostrophe and an "s" to a noun (e.g., Sarah's phone). For plural nouns ending in "s", you usually just add the apostrophe at the end (e.g., the teachers' lounge).
    Creating Contractions: It joins two words together by replacing omitted letters (e.g., "do not" becomes don't; "it is" becomes it's).
    Omitting Numbers: It can represent missing numbers in dates or slang (e.g., the '90s instead of the 1990s). 

Common Mistake to Avoid
Never use an apostrophe to make a standard noun plural. For example, writing "apple's for sale" is incorrect; it should simply be "apples".

1. The Weight Distribution Problem (Avoiding the Cugnot Crash)
Cugnot’s trike failed because the giant copper boiler hung entirely over the single front steering wheel, making it insanely unstable and impossible to turn at its blazing top speed of 2 mph. 

    The Primate Solution: Instead of one massive boiler upfront, your talking apes use their modular growth system. They split the weight by placing smaller, interconnected copper "satellite boilers" evenly along the flanks of the wooden chassis. This keeps the center of gravity low and perfectly balanced across multiple heavy, wide wooden rollers.

2. Low-Pressure "Atmospheric" Stepper Engines
Because high-pressure steam metallurgy took a long time to master historically, your primates rely on Newcomen's early logic: atmospheric pressure. 

    Instead of using steam to violently push a piston forward (which risks exploding a soft copper boiler), they fill a copper cylinder with steam and then inject cold water to condense it. 
    This creates a vacuum inside, and the weight of the outside air pushes the massive piston down. 
    Because these strokes are slow and rhythmic, the apes use a ratchet and pawl mechanism—similar to the one Cugnot used—to convert that slow, pulsing up-and-down movement into rotational energy to turn the tank's giant wheels.

    Copper Splice Plates: The wood is backed by thick plates of soft, malleable copper. When the tank rams a castle wall, the copper doesn't shatter like iron might; it deforms and crushes, absorbing the kinetic energy and protecting the fragile steam pipes and boilers behind it.

4. Primate Agility as the Braking System
The fardier à vapeur completely lacked a functional braking system. For a 100-tonne vehicle, mechanical brakes made of pre-1853 materials would simply friction-burn and disintegrate. 

    The "Anchor-Drop" Crew: Your primate crew takes advantage of their upper-body strength and climbing agility. Yes, Cugnot’s machine famously used a massive, globular copper boiler that looked like a giant cooking pot hanging off the front. Copper was the go-to material because it conducts heat beautifully, is easy to hammer into shape, and doesn't rust away like early iron. However, soft copper creates a major bottleneck for a heavy vehicle—it cannot safely hold high-pressure steam without bursting, meaning Cugnot was stuck operating at low pressures around 30 to 60 PSI. 
Since your fantasy primate world has an abundance of copper, they will face these exact real-world engineering constraints. Here is how your talking apes can manipulate copper to scale their tank up safely:
1. The Multi-Boiler "Honeycomb" System
A single, massive 100-tonne copper boiler would rupture under its own weight and steam pressure. To bypass this, your primates can build a modular system of dozens of small, interconnected copper boilers woven into the tank’s wooden framework. 

    Smaller copper vessels have a much higher natural resistance to pressure than one giant pot.
    If one boiler gets damaged or ruptures during a siege, the crew can isolate it using copper valves while the other boilers keep the tank moving.

2. Rivets and Silver Solder Work
Because copper cannot easily be welded with pre-1853 technology, the apes must rely on riveting and silver soldering to seal the plates. 

    With their high manual dexterity and nimbleness, teams of primates can work inside the cramped, growing wooden hull to hammer thousands of overlapping copper rivets.
    They can seal the seams with silver-copper brazing alloys to ensure the joints don't soften and leak when the wood fires are roaring hot. 

3. Fire-Tube Innovation
Historically, early boilers were just "big kettles" heated from the bottom, which took forever to boil. Later, engineers put tubes through the water to pass hot exhaust gases, vastly increasing heating efficiency. 

    Since copper is highly malleable, your apes can easily roll out long internal copper tubes to pass right through the water tanks.
    This "fire-tube" design allows the tank to generate steam quickly, preventing the vehicle from having to stop every 15 minutes to rebuild pressure like the historical French prototypes did. 

4. Thick-Walled Castings for Pistons
While the boilers are hammered out of copper sheets, the high-wear parts like the pistons and cylinders can be thickly cast out of bronze (copper mixed with common tin). Bronze handles the constant friction of the moving pistons much better than soft, pure copper.

Choosing between bronze (copper + tin) and brass (copper + zinc) for this backyard, Fundi-style 100-tonne tank introduces a massive engineering dilemma. 
The choice drastically alters the tank's survivability, how often it explodes, and how easily a single person can fix it in a hut. Historically, our grandfathers faced these exact chemical and physical limits when building steam engines before cheap steel. 
🥉 The Result of Using Bronze (The Durability Route)
If your Fundi Priests choose bronze, they are building a tank designed for extreme wear, longevity, and safety. Historically, the classic blend was "gunmetal bronze" (copper, tin, and a bit of zinc). 

    The Safe "Vroom" Boiler: Bronze is stronger and tougher than brass under pressure. If the steam pot gets too hot, a bronze boiler will warp or deform slowly rather than violently tearing open. It holds up to live steam beautifully over decades. 
    The "Cleaver" Mortar Survives: Bronze handles the violent shockwaves of gunpowder significantly better. A short, fat bronze mortar can fire thousands of granite boulders without cracking. 
    Unstoppable Bearing Glides: Bronze has low metal-on-metal friction and acts as a stable wear surface. Even if the primates forget to grease the log axles with animal fat, the bronze bushings will keep sliding smoothly without locking up or melting down. 
    The Downside (The Foundry Nightmare): Tin is rare, and bronze has a high melting point. It flows sluggishly into pottery molds. A single ape in a hut will struggle to scrape or chisel a rough bronze casting because the metal fights back against hand tools. 

🟡 The Result of Using Brass (The Village Manufacturing Route)
If your Fundi Priests use brass, they are prioritizing easy village casting, fast machining, and massive, cheap volume at the cost of structural longevity. 

    The One-Man Handcrafted Victory: Brass melts at a lower temperature and flows smoothly like water into pottery molds. A single primate using basic stone chisels can easily scrape, smooth, and alter a brass casting right inside their longhouse.
    The "Dezincification" Boiler Trap: This is the major historical failure mode. Live steam and acidic water (especially from animal-manure runoff or stagnant swamp water) will chemically dissolve the zinc right out of the brass. Over time, the heavy cast-brass "Steam Pot" will secretly turn into a porous, weak copper sponge. Without warning, the boiler will suffer a catastrophic pressure failure.
    Brittle Mortar Explosions: Brass wears out faster under high-friction, high-impact environments. The intense heat and pressure of the homemade gunpowder will slowly micro-crack the interior of a brass mortar breech, turning your "cleaver" into a ticking time bomb.
    The "Smear and Seize" Axles: Under the crushing 100-tonne weight of the cedar hull, brass bearings tend to "smear" and seize if lubrication fails. The axles could lock up completely mid-battle if they run dry. 

⚖️ The Wise "Fundi" Verdict
To make this tank practically work in an African bushcraft style, the Copper Architects and Priests will use a hybrid strategy:

    The Core Engine & Mortar (Bronze): They will save their precious tin (or use natural arsenic-bronze) strictly for the mortar barrel and the piston cylinders. They need that brute-force durability so the tank doesn't blast itself apart.

Leaded bronze is the absolute silver bullet for this learner’s tank design. In fact, it is the exact material real-world historic engineers used for heavy machinery bearings and steam fittings before high-grade modern steels were invented. 
Historically, alloys like LG2 or SAE 660 (roughly 80–85% copper, 5–7% tin, and 5–7% lead) were known as the ultimate "forgiving metals". For your Fundi Copper Priests teaching clumsy young apprentices, leaded bronze acts as a literal safety net built directly into the atoms of the metal. 
Here is why leaded bronze makes the 10-tonne trainer perfectly functional, cheap, and educational:
1. The Low-Temperature Village Melt
Pure copper melts at a scorching 1,085°C and is famously terrible to cast because it bubbles and turns "gummy". 

    The Lead Fix: Adding lead and a small amount of tin drastically drops the melting point of the alloy.
    Liquid Flow: It makes the molten metal flow smoothly like river water into the crude pottery kilns built by the apes. It fills out the cylinder molds beautifully, preventing structural air bubbles even when cast by an uncoordinated apprentice. 

2. Built-In "Emergency Lubrication" (The Anti-Seize Miracle)
Young learners will inevitably forget to grease the engine cylinders with animal tallow. In a pure copper or high-tin bronze engine, a dry piston will immediately friction-weld itself to the cylinder wall, violently snapping the cedar log drivetrain.

    Microscopic Grease: Lead doesn't actually dissolve into copper; when it cools, it forms microscopic, isolated pockets of pure, soft lead throughout the harder copper-tin matrix. 
    Self-Healing Wear: If an apprentice runs the engine dry and it begins to overheat, the tiny pockets of lead will slightly bleed to the surface under the intense friction. This creates a temporary, self-lubricating film that prevents the piston from seizing up. The engine will just hiss and slow down, giving the Fundi Priest time to smack the apprentice upside the head and tell them to add more grease. 

3. The Khyber Pass "Chisel-Friendly" Factor
High-quality gunmetal bronze is incredibly tough and difficult to cut by hand. If a young ape casts a rough cylinder, scraping the inside smooth with stone tools would take weeks.

    Chips, Not Strings: The lead particles act as a "chip breaker". When a primate apprentice uses a salvaged Japanese iron chisel to clean up the inside of the piston bore, the leaded bronze easily flakes away in neat, crisp chunks instead of peeling off in long, gummy, frustrating ribbons. It allows a single person in a hut to finish a cylinder using primitive hand tools.

There is a catch that the Fundi Copper Priests must teach the young learners with absolute gravity: toxic fumes. 

    When leaded bronze is melted in the charcoal pit, the lead can vaporize, producing a sweet-smelling but highly toxic heavy-metal smoke.
    The Priests teach the young apprentices that these castings can only be poured in open-air huts with a strong cross-breeze.
