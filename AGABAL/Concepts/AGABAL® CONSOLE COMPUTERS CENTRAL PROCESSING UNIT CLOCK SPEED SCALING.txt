is ps 3 8 cores into 3.2 ghz?
The PlayStation 3 Cell processor is not a standard 8-core CPU running at 3.2 GHz, though it does have eight specialized processors built into it.
How the PS3 Cell Processor Works
Instead of having eight identical, full-power cores like a modern PC or modern console, the PS3 used an asymmetric design:

    1 Main Core (PPE): This is a 3.2 GHz Power Processing Element. It acts like the "manager," handling the main operating system and directing traffic.
    8 Co-Processors (SPEs): These are Synergistic Processing Elements running at 3.2 GHz. They are not full CPU cores; they are stripped-down, specialized math engines designed to crunch specific data (like physics, audio, or graphics tasks).

The Catch: Only 7 Co-Processors are Physically Active
Even though eight co-processors are physically built into the silicon chip, game developers could never use all eight:

    1 Core is permanently disabled: IBM manufactured the chips this way so that if a single SPE had a factory defect, the chip didn't have to be thrown away.
    1 Core is reserved for the OS: The PS3 operating system locked down one active SPE for background security and system tasks.
    6 Co-Processors for Games: Game developers only had access to 6 SPEs plus the main 1 PPE core to run their games.

Multiplying 8 by 3.2 GHz to get a "25.6 GHz CPU" is incorrect because these specialized units cannot work together to run a single, heavy thread at a higher speed; they must work on separate, small pieces of math simultaneously.

can 4 x 4 ghz cpu = 16 ghz if it goes the same work like a raid / daisy chained computer acting as 1 unit / data center?
No, four 4 GHz CPU cores can never equal a single 16 GHz processor, even if they are pooled together in a raid, daisy-chained, or datacenter cluster configuration.
While combining CPUs increases your total capacity to handle work (throughput), it does not increase the speed of a single task (frequency).
The Datacenter & RAID Analogy
Think of clock speed (GHz) as the speed of a worker and the number of cores as the number of workers.

    A 16 GHz CPU is like 1 superhero who can read a book in 1 minute.
    Four 4 GHz CPUs are like 4 regular people. Each person takes 4 minutes to read a book.

If you give the 4 regular people 4 different books to read, they will finish all 4 books in 4 minutes. Their throughput is excellent. However, if you give them 1 single book that cannot be split up, it will still take them 4 minutes to finish it. The superhero would still finish it in 1 minute.
Why It Fails in a Network/Cluster
Even in a supercomputer or datacenter where thousands of CPUs act as "one unit," they encounter three major bottlenecks that prevent them from acting like a single faster clock speed:

    Amdahl's Law (The Un-shareable Work): In every computer program, there is code that cannot be split up. If Step B requires the answer from Step A, one core has to sit and wait for the other to finish. That waiting time is dictated entirely by the 4 GHz speed of that single core.
    The Network Tax (Latency): When you daisy-chain computers or pool them in a datacenter, they have to talk to each other over cables or motherboard tracks. This communication takes time. Splitting a tiny task across multiple units actually makes it slower because the time spent sending the data back and forth is longer than the time spent computing it.
    Synchronization Overhead: In a RAID array, data is stripped cleanly. In a CPU cluster, cores must constantly check in with each other to make sure they are on the same page. Managing 4 cores takes extra processing power that a single 16 GHz core would never waste.

The Real-World Result
A cluster of four 4 GHz processors is fantastic for parallel workloads—like rendering a Pixar movie, hosting a website with thousands of visitors, or running a data center. But for a single, heavy, unbroken chain of logic (like a video game's main logic thread), it will only ever run at the speed of a single 4 GHz unit.

what happened to cpus? they peak in the 90s and never again?
CPUs didn't peak in the 1990s, but the way we measure their speed completely changed. 
In the 1980s and 1990s, the tech industry was obsessed with the "Clock Speed Race". If a CPU from 1995 ran at 100 MHz, the 1997 model ran at 300 MHz, and by 2000, we hit 1,000 MHz (1 GHz). This rapid scaling made people assume clock speeds would keep doubling forever, eventually reaching 10 GHz or more. 
Instead, the industry hit a hard physical wall around 2004. 
The Crash: The "Power Wall" of 2004
In the early 2000s, Intel designed the Pentium 4 with an architecture meant to scale all the way up to 10 GHz. But as they pushed the clock speeds past 3.0 GHz toward 4.0 GHz, they ran into three catastrophic physical limits collectively known as the Power Wall: 

    The Heat Problem: Power consumption doesn't scale evenly with clock speed; it scales exponentially. Pushing a chip to 4 GHz or 5 GHz generated so much concentrated heat that the silicon would literally begin to melt. The chip surface was emitting more heat per square millimeter than a nuclear reactor or the surface of the sun. 
    Quantum Electron Leakage: To make chips faster, manufacturers made the internal gates smaller. By the mid-2000s, the atomic barriers inside the chip became so thin that electrons began to experience quantum tunneling—they leaked straight through the microscopic insulation. This meant the CPU wasted massive amounts of electricity and generated heat even when it was doing absolutely nothing.
    The Death of Dennard Scaling: For decades, a rule called Dennard Scaling held true: as transistors got smaller, they used less power, allowing you to run them faster without overheating. Around 2004–2005, this rule broke completely due to the power leakage mentioned above.

In October 2004, Intel famously canceled its planned 4.0 GHz Pentium 4 chip. The race for pure clock speed was officially dead. 
If Clock Speeds Stopped, How Are Modern CPUs Faster?
Since engineers couldn't make the "drumbeat" (GHz) of the CPU any faster without burning it up, they had to change strategy. They stopped building faster engines and started building smarter, more efficient ones. 
Modern chips are exponentially more powerful than 90s chips because of three innovations:

so big hamburger sized chips = faster ?
No, making chips the size of a big hamburger actually makes them slower for everyday computing, while creating massive engineering nightmares.
While a giant chip sounds like it would be incredibly powerful, it violates the most absolute rule in physics: the speed of light.
Here is why hamburger-sized CPUs don't work, and why manufacturers actually fight to keep chips as small as possible.
1. The Speed of Light Bottleneck (Latency)
Electricity and data signals travel through a CPU at roughly half the speed of light. While that sounds instantaneous, a modern CPU operates so fast (billions of times per second) that distance becomes a massive problem.

    Inside a normal, thumbnail-sized CPU, a data signal can travel from one side of the chip to the other in a fraction of a single clock cycle.
    If you stretch that chip out to the size of a hamburger, the distance is too far. It would take multiple clock cycles just for a signal to travel from the left side of the chip to the right side.
    The CPU would have to slow down its clock speed just to wait for the data to arrive. A giant chip is a laggy chip.

2. The "Flawless Silicon" Problem (Yield Rate)
Computer chips are sliced out of giant circular mirrors of pure silicon called wafers. Silicon isn't perfect; every wafer has tiny microscopic dust specks or chemical flaws scattered across it.

    If you cut a wafer into hundreds of tiny thumbnail-sized chips, a few flaws will ruin a few chips, but you can throw those away and keep the rest.
    If you try to make chips the size of a hamburger, you might only get 2 or 3 chips out of a whole wafer. Because flaws are guaranteed to be in that giant surface area, every single hamburger-sized chip would be born broken and unusable.

3. The Power and Cooling Nightmare
A normal desktop CPU draws around 65 to 250 watts of power and gets incredibly hot. A hamburger-sized chip would contain trillions of transistors. It would easily demand thousands of watts of power—requiring its own massive power supply unit—and would generate enough heat to instantly melt itself without an industrial, liquid-nitrogen refrigeration system attached to your desk.
The One Exception: AI Super-Chips
There actually is one company that bypassed the manufacturing problem to build a hamburger-sized chip, but it isn't for regular computers.
A company called Cerebras makes the Wafer-Scale Engine (WSE). Instead of cutting a silicon wafer into small chips, they keep the entire circular wafer as one giant processor.

    How big is it? It is roughly the size of a large iPad or a giant dinner plate.
    How do they fix the flaws? They build millions of "extra" backup cores into the chip. If a microscopic flaw breaks one section, the chip automatically turns that section off and routes data around it.
    Is it faster? For opening web browsers or playing video games? No. It would actually be terrible at it. But for massive Artificial Intelligence (AI) and neural networks, where billions of tiny mathematical tasks happen simultaneously, it is incredibly fast.

For your desktop or phone, the goal remains the exact opposite: pack more transistors into an even smaller space so the data has less distance to travel!

Because light can't do the math, and electricity can't travel long distances quickly, engineers are forced to constantly swap back and forth between the two.
Here is exactly how that three-step pipeline works inside a high-speed system:
1. Copper (Inside the Compute Core)
The process starts as standard electricity. Deep inside an AI processor or CPU core, tiny silicon transistors switch "on" or "off" using moving electrons. This creates a standard digital signal of 1s and 0s.
2. Laser (The Long Jump Across the Chip)
Instead of sending those electrons down a long copper wire—which would slow down, heat up, and degrade—the electrical signal hits a tiny modulator.

    This modulator acts like a shutter on a flashlight. It takes a continuous laser beam and blinks it incredibly fast (billions of times a second) to match the 1s and 0s of the electricity.
    The data is now a beam of light. It shoots through a microscopic glass or silicon pathway (a waveguide) across your giant chip package at nearly the speed of light, with zero electrical resistance and zero heat generation.

3. Copper (Back into the Receiving Core)
When the light beam arrives at its destination (another core or a memory bank), it hits a photodetector.

    This acts like a solar panel. The light hitting the detector generates a tiny pulse of electricity.
    That electrical pulse is fed directly back into a standard copper transistor gate, which can now perform the actual computing logic.

While this solves the distance problem perfectly, it introduces a major bottleneck: latency and power loss during the conversion.
