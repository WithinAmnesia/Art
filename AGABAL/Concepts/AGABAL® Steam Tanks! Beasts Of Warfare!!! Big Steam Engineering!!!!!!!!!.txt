how much psi does a 10 tonne steam tractor need to move it self around? at like 10 kph
To move itself around on level ground at 10 kph, a 10-tonne steam tractor typically requires a boiler pressure of 100 to 150 PSI, though it can physically crawl forward on as little as 40 to 50 PSI. 
The pressure needed is not just about overcoming the tractor's weight, but about overcoming rolling friction, maintaining engine RPM, and sustaining the massive volume of steam required to travel at top speed. 
⚙️ Why 10 kph Changes the Equation

    The Speed Limit: For a heavy, steel-wheeled 10-tonne agricultural steam tractor, 10 kph (approx. 6 mph) is incredibly fast—effectively its top road-gear speed.
    The Pressure Requirement: While you only need about 45–50 PSI to break static friction and slowly hostle (creep) the tractor forward, maintaining 10 kph requires the engine to spin at its maximum rated RPM (usually 200–250 RPM). To keep up with that rapid mechanical cycle without the engine stalling out from rolling resistance, the boiler needs to stay well within its standard working pressure of 120–150 PSI. 

⚖️ Pressure vs. Volume (The Real Restriction)
In steam engineering, pressure equals torque (starting force), but volume equals speed. 

    At low speeds: If you just want to move the tractor 50 feet across a flat yard, the pressure can drop down to 40 PSI. The piston will still stroke because it has enough surface area to push the gears. 
    At 10 kph: The pistons are demanding steam at an extreme volumetric rate. If your boiler pressure drops too low, the steam loses its expansive force. The throttle and governor won't be able to feed the cylinders fast enough to overcome the heavy rolling resistance of those massive steel wheels. 

🚜 Typical Historical Pressure Limits

    Maximum Factory Working Pressure: Most historic farm traction engines were built to operate at a maximum of 150 to 175 PSI when brand new. 
    Modern Safety Regulations: Today, most historical boiler associations and government inspectors limit these vintage boilers to a maximum of 100 to 140 PSI for public safety and to reduce stress on antique steel.

⚙️ How Wheel Modifications Impact the Steam Tractor
Swapping standard steel wheels for tracks or dreadnaught wheels actually lowers the steam tractor's top speed, making it lose to the tanks by an even wider margin. Here is how those setups behave at high stress:

    Dreadnaught Wheels (Pedrail Wheels): These wheels use hinged wooden or steel "shoes" attached to the rim to act as a continuous portable track. At 10 kph, the centrifugal force and violent slapping of the heavy shoes against the ground would cause catastrophic mechanical failure.     Continuous Tracks: Converting a steam tractor into a tracked vehicle (like an early Holt or Hornsby steam crawler) massively increases rolling resistance and friction. Because a steam engine relies on a limited volume of steam, the heavy mechanical drag of a track system saps the engine's RPM, dropping its top speed down to roughly 3 to 5 kph.

    Locomotion Mode: The clutch engages the heavy bull-gears driving the Heathcote tracks or Boydell dreadnaught wheels.
