An apostrophe (') is a punctuation mark used primarily to show possession or to indicate omitted letters in contractions. 
🛠️ Primary Uses of the Apostrophe

    Contractions: Replaces missing letters when shortening word combinations (e.g., "do not" becomes "don't", "we will" becomes "we'll").
    Singular Possession: Shows ownership by adding an apostrophe and an "s" to singular nouns (e.g., "Mary's car").
    Plural Possession: Shows ownership for plural nouns ending in "s" by adding just the apostrophe at the end (e.g., "the players' facility").
    Omitted Time: Replaces missing numbers in years or centuries (e.g., "'97" instead of 1997). 

⚠️ Common Mistakes to Avoid

    Plural nouns: Do not use an apostrophe to make a regular word plural (e.g., write "cats," not "cat's").
    Possessive pronouns: Words like "its," "hers," and "yours" never take an apostrophe to show ownership.

📜 The History of Steam Horsepower Metrics
When James Watt popularized the steam engine in the late 1700s, he needed a way to market his machines to mill owners who relied on draft animals. He calculated that a single brewery horse could comfortably do roughly 75 kilogram-meters of work per second. This commercial marketing tool created the universal metric for power, which later split into two primary definitions as industrialization spread across the globe:

    Imperial Horsepower (hp): Used predominantly in the British Empire and the United States. It is based strictly on imperial units and is defined as the power required to lift 550 pounds vertically by 1 foot in exactly 1 second (~745.7 Watts).
    Metric Horsepower (PS / Pferdestärke): Developed in continental Europe to standardize Watt's calculation with the metric system. It is defined as the power required to lift 75 kilograms vertically by 1 meter in exactly 1 second (~735.5 Watts).

Because 1 hp is equal to 1.014 PS, they are incredibly close, but international engineering documents prioritize PS for metric machinery datasets.

    Design Profile: Open-topped wooden log walls bolted onto a basic 4.0-meter 10-tonne tracked frame. Features no turret or rotating rings. A massive, short-barreled bronze mortar is bolted directly to the center floorboards.
    The Control Meta: Driven by a dead-simple Two-Lever Clutch-and-Brake System. Pushing both levers sends steam directly to both track engines; pulling one lever clamps a raw leather band brake onto that axle to pivot. It features no complex gearboxes or fragile transmission arrays.
    The Common Sense Reality: It is cheap, expendable, and requires zero advanced factory nodes. Factions can build hundreds of these "Battle Barns" in small field camps using local timber and scrap copper. They drive up to a trench, fire an overpressure payload, and back away.

🚂 2. The "Vickers-Sloop" Medium Cruiser (The Line Standard — Level 40/100)

    Design Profile: Standardized 8.0-meter chassis. The boiler, firebox, and fuel bays are placed completely in the rear of the vehicle. The driver sits upfront, and a centralized grease-lubricated wooden turntable ring sits on the roof hull.
    The Evolutionary Logic: Moving the engine to the rear perfectly counter-balances the immense weight of the forward armament while protecting the crew from sudden boiler blowbacks if the front hull takes a hit. The roof ring carries a 1-Kilogrammer Automatic Steam Cannon. High-pressure steam is tapped straight from the boiler manifold and injected into the breech alongside low-velocity powder, triggering a pneumatic machine-gun effect that tears through enemy wooden wood-and-clay armor.

🛡️ 3. The "Sloped Turtle" Turtle Tank (The Late-War Deflection Meta — Level 70/100)

    Design Profile: Features radical, extreme sloped hulls. Flat, vertical wooden or iron walls are completely discarded. The frontal glacis is constructed out of heavy logs cut at sharp 45 to 60-degree angles, capped with thin, overlapping copper plates.
    The Evolutionary Logic: Because incoming enemy black powder projectiles hit with very low kinetic velocity due to the 3x chemical penalty, they cannot punch through targets via brute force. A 60-degree sloped wooden plate completely negates their momentum, forcing round shot to harmlessly glance off and bounce away. This allows players to achieve maximum defense metrics using light, cheap materials instead of hauling rare steel plate armor.

🏛️ 4. The "Segmented Citadel" Dreadnaught (The Endgame Monument — Level 100/100)

    Design Profile: A colossal 32.0-meter wide, multi-module rolling city. To prevent a vehicle of this size from instantly snapping its frame on uneven terrain, it is engineered as an Articulated Centipede. It consists of four independent 2,500-tonne modules connected by massive copper-braided flexible rope cables and heavy bronze hinge pins, rolling on 24 independent Boydell wheel bogies.
    The Common Sense Reality: Like an Age of Sail Ship of the Line, every single Level 100/100 fortress is unique. They are not spit out of automated factories; they are built over weeks of server-wide clan efforts. They feature a mismatched patchwork of eras—the left bastion might be built of pristine high-tier black steel plate, while the right tower is a patched-up block keep thrown together after a siege. It acts as a mobile RTS base, driving heavy timber piles directly into the bedrock to anchor itself down on the frontline to refine ore and craft munitions for the faction.

    The Tactical Risk: The vehicle is a sitting duck while the solo player is reloading. It is mechanically functional but highly inefficient.

2. Hired Primate Bots (The OSRS Sailing Blueprint)

    Solo players or small teams can fill missing crew seats by hiring automated tribal primate bots, paid continuously using food rations or copper scrap.

    When a full team of human players occupies dedicated stations, the vehicle transformed into an unstoppable, lethal weapon system.

    The Gunner continuously tracks targets through the sight brackets.
    The Loader works the ammo cranes to achieve maximum possible fire rates.
    The Engineer stands in the bowels of the machine, actively balancing water gauges and running around with a wooden mallet to drive fresh wood wedges into smoking hull fractures to patch breaches mid-combat.

4. The Flywheel Kinetic Dump

    A fully human crew unlocks advanced mechanical maneuvers that bots cannot execute. When a fortress encounters a castle wall or a heavy barricade, the driver and engineer coordinate perfectly to disengage the drivetrain and run the 800 hp steam engine unthrottled, spinning the massive internal cast-iron flywheel up to maximum velocity.
    Upon impact, the driver slams a heavy wedge-clutch directly into the axle gear. The entire stored rotational inertia of the flywheel dumps into the tracks all at once, forcing the massive timber hull to violently surge forward, using pure kinetic momentum to ram straight through stone defenses without stalling the boiler pistons.
