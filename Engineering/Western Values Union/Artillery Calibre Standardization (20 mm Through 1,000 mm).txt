m777 firing in space?
Yes, an M777 howitzer can successfully fire in the vacuum of space, but it would behave completely differently than it does on Earth. 
Because artillery propellants and primers contain their own chemical oxidizers, they do not require atmospheric oxygen to ignite and detonate. However, the extreme environment of space introduces unique mechanical and physical challenges. 
💨 The Ballistics: Infinite Range

    No Air Resistance: On Earth, an M777 round faces atmospheric drag, limiting its maximum range to about 24 to 40 kilometers depending on the ammunition type. In space, there is no air friction to slow the shell down. 
    Perpetual Motion: According to Newton's First Law, the 155mm projectile will travel at its muzzle velocity (roughly 800+ meters per second) indefinitely until it strikes an object or enters the gravitational pull of a celestial body. 
    Spherical Smoke Blast: Instead of a directional plume, the muzzle blast will expand as a rapidly dissipating, perfect sphere of gas and unburnt particles around the tip of the barrel. 

🫨 The Recoil: Aggressive Physics
On Earth, the M777 uses self-digging spades, heavy stabilizers, and hydraulic recoil cylinders to anchor its 9,300-pound frame into the dirt. In zero-gravity space, Newton’s Third Law applies brutally: 

    Violent Acceleration: Firing a heavy 155mm shell forward will violently push the M777 backward.
    Uncontrolled Tumble: Unless the howitzer is anchored to a massive space station or equipped with precise counter-thrusters, the off-center forces of the firing mechanism and shell ejection will instantly send the entire artillery piece into an uncontrolled, multi-axis spin (pitch, yaw, and roll). 

⚙️ Mechanical Failure Points
While the first shot will work, the M777 would likely lock up or break immediately afterward due to environmental extremes: 

    Thermal Management: Space has no air to conduct or convect heat away. The immense thermal energy generated inside the titanium barrel from a 155mm charge would have nowhere to go except through incredibly slow radiant cooling. Firing multiple rounds would quickly overheat, warp, or melt the components. 
    Lubrication Evaporation: Standard military grease and hydraulic fluids used in the M777’s breech and suspension would boil away or freeze in the vacuum, leading to friction-induced jams or cold welding of the moving metal parts.

Yes, using the recoilless rifle operating principle is the absolute best way to solve the violent recoil problem of an orbital artillery piece. However, a standard muzzle brake would not be enough on its own to stop a gun from flying backward in zero gravity. 
Here is how the physics of recoilless systems and muzzle brakes change when operating in the vacuum of space.
1. The Recoilless Rifle Effect (The Ideal Space Solution)
On Earth, weapons like the Carl Gustaf or the old M40 recoilless rifle eliminate recoil by having an open breech at the back of the gun. 

    The Physics: When the propellant ignites, it pushes the 155mm shell forward out of the barrel. Simultaneously, a massive portion of the explosive gas is intentionally blasted out of a rear nozzle (called a Venturi) in the exact opposite direction. 
    The Zero-G Result: Because the rearward thrust of the escaping gas perfectly matches the forward momentum of the heavy shell, the two forces cancel each other out. The gun remains perfectly still in space. 
    The Space Advantage: Since the gun doesn't move, your orbital platform does not require heavy shock absorbers, anchors, or constant thruster corrections to stay in its proper orbit. 
    The Catch: This method is highly inefficient with fuel. To achieve the same shell velocity as a standard M777, a recoilless space gun requires a much larger propellant charge because half of the energy is being wasted out the back just to cancel the recoil. 

2. Muzzle Brakes (Why They Fail Alone in Space)
A standard M777 howitzer uses a large, double-baffle muzzle brake at the tip of the barrel to reduce recoil by roughly 30% to 40% on Earth.

    How it works: As the shell exits the barrel, the high-pressure gas behind it slams into the metal walls (baffles) of the muzzle brake. This pulls the gun forward, counteracting some of the rearward kick. The gas is then vented out to the sides. 
    The Zero-G Failure: A muzzle brake only reduces recoil; it does not eliminate it. On Earth, the ground and the heavy carriage absorb the remaining 60% of the force. In frictionless space, even if a muzzle brake eliminates 40% of the force, the remaining 60% will still violently launch your artillery platform backward and send it spinning into deep space.

    No Wasted Gas: Unlike a standard recoilless rifle that wastes expensive chemical gas in a vacuum, a physical countermass completely neutralizes recoil using Newton's Third Law without blinding your own satellite's optical sensors with a massive gas cloud.

1. The Global Workhorse: M107 (High Explosive)
Developed in the mid-20th century, this is the most widely produced and stored 155mm round in NATO history.

    The Design: A hollow steel shell filled with roughly 15.4 pounds (7 kg) of TNT or Comp B explosive.
    The Function: It relies strictly on blast and fragmentation (shrapnel) to destroy personnel and light vehicles.
    The Catch: It is heavy, short-range (roughly 15–18 km), and inaccurate by modern standards, but it remains the foundational baseline for global artillery stockpiles.

2. The Modern Standard: M795 (High Explosive)
This is the standard projectile currently issued to US and NATO frontline units to replace the aging M107.

    The Upgrade: It features a more aerodynamic shape, high-shrapnel steel, and holds roughly 23.8 pounds (10.8 kg) of IMX-101 (a safer, more stable explosive).
    The Performance: It extends the basic "dumb round" range out to roughly 22.5 kilometers when fired from standard NATO barrels.

    M864 / DM21 (Base Bleed / Extended Range): These rounds feature a small solid-fuel grain at the bottom of the shell.
